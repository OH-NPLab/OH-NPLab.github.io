01

Nutritional Psychology & Mental Health

How do food and dietary patterns intersect with emotion, cognition and psychological well-being?

+

We examine nutrition as part of a reciprocal psychological system rather than as an isolated biological exposure. This includes the relationship between diet and mental health, affective processes surrounding eating, and pathways through which the gut–brain axis, inflammation and everyday dietary patterns may connect physical and psychological experience.

02

Orthorexia Nervosa

When does the pursuit of healthy eating become rigid, identity-laden or clinically significant?

+

Our orthorexia research asks where health-oriented eating ends and psychologically costly rigidity begins. We study measurement, risk factors and clinical implications, while also examining perfectionism, childhood eating patterns, identity and the cultural meanings attached to dietary purity. The aim is to understand orthorexia without reducing healthy eating itself to pathology.

03

Theory of Food

How do early food experiences become enduring preferences, attitudes and dietary identities?

+

Theory of Food is an integrative framework for understanding how food exploration and childhood eating habits may leave durable psychological traces. We investigate how early exposure, selectivity and implicit attitudes relate to later food preferences and dietary choices, and how these developmental processes may differ across people and dietary groups.

04

Eating Disorders, Identity & Morality

How do eating practices become entangled with identity, values, agency and moral ideas of health?

+

Eating is often more than behaviour: it can become a way of organizing identity, regulating the self and expressing moral commitments. This line of research examines restrictive eating and eating-disorder presentations through affective neuroscience, identity processes and the moralization of health, with particular attention to the different functions similar eating behaviours may serve.

05

Interoception, Selfhood & Affective Neuroscience

How might the temporal organization of bodily rhythms help stabilize the continuity of self-experience?

+

This emerging line of research returns to foundational questions in affective neuroscience and embodied selfhood. We are developing the Interoceptive Pacing Hypothesis: a working proposal that temporally coordinated viscero-somatic rhythms may provide a bodily-temporal anchor for minimal self-experience. The framework asks whether the timing and coordination of cardiac, respiratory, visceral and neural signals contribute not only to regulation, but to the felt continuity of a first-person perspective. It opens a research program spanning dissociation and altered states, developmental and hormonal transitions, and experimentally measurable brain–body coupling.

Current workHorovitz, O., & Dahan, O. Interoceptive Pacing and the Temporal Organization of Minimal Selfhood: A Working Proposal for a Layered Model of Affective Experience. Manuscript under review.
